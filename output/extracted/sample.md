### 1. About Fluid Intelligence

#### 1.1 What Fluid Intelligence is

Fluid Intelligence is a behavioural design and technology consulting firm that helps organisations become AI-native by reimagining how they think, work and build AI people trust.

In plain words: we help organisations get real use out of AI. There are three ways in. We raise what your people can do with AI. We rebuild one everyday process with AI inside it. Or we design and build a new AI product with you. We combine design, AI and behavioural science, and we build as well as advise. You do not get a deck about the work; you get the work: people who can do more, a process that runs differently, or a working product. For deep AI engineering, engineers from Trinos work inside our team.

Fluid Intelligence was founded in July 2026 by Ramchand Matta. We are based in India and work with teams anywhere.

#### 1.2 What our name means

Fluid intelligence is the psychologists’ term for the ability to reason through unfamiliar problems, connect ideas that have never met, and keep learning. Its opposite, crystallised intelligence, is what you already know.

For decades, success was built on crystallised intelligence: the store of knowledge, expertise and experience a person builds up. Now AI can find information instantly and generate answers in seconds, so knowledge alone no longer sets anyone apart. What matters is fluid intelligence: asking better questions, adapting to new situations, thinking across disciplines, and creating solutions for problems that have never existed before. That is the capability we help people and organisations build.

AI is very good at what is already known. It makes the other kind of intelligence more valuable, not less. We named the company after that human capability on purpose, and not after a technology, because anything named after the technology of the moment dates with it.

We believe AI does not replace human intelligence. It amplifies it. Our role is to help people combine human creativity, judgement and empathy with AI to solve problems that matter.

#### 1.3 Our tagline

**Think fluid. Create together.**

*Think fluid* is the human ability our company is named after: reasoning through what you have not met before. *Create together* is the murmuration, our idea that intelligence is something people create between them, not only inside one mind (see 7.2 and 7.4).

#### 1.4 Our vision

**A world where intelligence is fluid: where humans and AI learn, adapt and create what neither could alone.**

Every generation of this technology will be more capable than the last. We want a world where people are too. Where a student choosing her future, a leader deciding for thousands, a teacher, a designer, a founder each meet AI as something that raises them. The other future is already being built, politely, by default: systems designed around what can be automated, and people designed around the systems. We exist to make the first future normal.

“Leave people more capable than we found them” is the promise every engagement ends with. Most organisations buy fluency for their people, a product, and a story from three different suppliers. ### 3. The problem we solve

#### 3.1 Why AI spending brings so little back

Organisations are spending real money on AI and getting very little back.

The usual explanation is that the technology is not ready yet. That is not what we see. What we see is that organisations need two things to be true at the same time:

- **AI literacy**: what your people can do. Not whether they have heard of the tools, but whether they know when to reach for AI, when not to, and how to judge what it hands back.
- **AI maturity**: what your organisation can support. The eight dimensions of AI maturity: your data, technology and operations; your strategy, skills, culture and rules; and whether you can show that any of it worked.

Most organisations build only one. Train your people without building the organisation and you get frustration. Build the organisation without training your people and you get waste. Almost nobody is stuck because the technology does not work.

How we see it: the spend is not wasted, it is unactivated. The tools usually work; what is missing is the habit, the rules or the process around them.

How we help: we measure both things at once, in one week (see the AI Readiness Assessment), so you know which one is short. Then we work on the one that is short, inside the work your people already do: raising what your people can do (see Build Fluid Minds), fixing the rules, workflows and ownership that stop them (see Build Intelligent Organisations), or building the thing the readiness was for (see Build Emergent Products). We start from the AI you have already paid for; most of our work is getting more out of tools you own, not adding new ones.

#### 3.2 Problems too big for one person, one kind of expert, one organisation or one machine

The problems worth solving are too big for one person, one kind of expert, one organisation or one machine. Climate. Healthcare. Education. Banking. Insurance. Retail. In every one of these, the work that matters needs people from different disciplines and AI working together.

So buying AI is not enough. Your people and your organisation have to work with it, together. That is why we work on both at once: what your people can do, and what your organisation can support.

How we help: we bring the different disciplines and the AI into the same piece of work. A Team AI Residency puts our people inside one of your teams for a month, solving that team’s real problems with AI and writing down what worked (see Build Fluid Minds). Our product studio puts strategy, design and engineering in one team, with engineers from Trinos inside it (see Build Emergent Products). And our collaboration with Trinos is itself an example: design, research and behavioural science on one side, AI and platform engineering on the other, working as one team for the client.

#### 3.3 Two questions, four zones

Two questions tell you where your organisation stands with AI. Are your people ready to use AI? Can your organisation support it?

The first question is about AI literacy, what your people can do. The second is about AI maturity, what your organisation can support. Put the two together and there are four places an organisation can be standing:

| Zone                        | People · Organisation                     | What is there                                                                                           |
|-----------------------------|-------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Hype Zone**               | People not ready · Organisation not ready | Lack of AI skills. Minimal AI strategy, governance and adoption. AI is misused or ignored.              |
| **Frustration Zone**        | People ready · Organisation not ready     | Off-the-record AI use, no support. Lack of governance, operations and alignment.                        |
| **Wasted Opportunity Zone** | People not ready · Organisation ready     | Investment in platforms, governance and strategy. Workforce is untrained and resists adoption.          |
| **Transformation Zone**     | People ready · Organisation ready         | Employees know how, when and which AI to use. Appropriate technology, data and operations are in place. |

The AI readiness zones and the eight dimensions of AI maturity come from *The AI Maturity Benchmark* by Chris McKay and Jess Conser. We use their framework with credit. The way out of each zone, and everything we offer against it, is ours.

How we use the zones: a zone is not a label we hang on you. It is an instruction. It says which of the two things is short, your people or your organisation, and therefore what to do first. The AI Readiness Assessment places you on the map and names the one or two dimensions holding everything else back. Then we make one of three moves: raise the people, fix the organisation, or build the new thing (see our method). Each zone below ends with what we do in it.

#### 3.4 The Hype Zone

The Hype Zone is where an organisation stands when its people are not yet ready to use AI and the organisation is not yet set up to support it. Without readiness, AI hype rarely translates into value.

What it looks like: AI is on the agenda, but not in the system. People lack the skills to use AI effectively. The organisation lacks the tools, strategy and governance to support adoption.

What it feels like: high urgency, low commitment. Leaders recognise AI’s potential, but investment in people and organisational capabilities has not followed. AI is discussed, experimented with sporadically, or ignored.

What it costs you: spend without meaningful change. AI investments become reactive, often driven by competitor activity rather than business needs. Adoption stalls, workflows remain unchanged, and organisations see little difference.

In plain words: nothing has gone wrong yet, and nothing is in place yet either. That is a fair place to start from. The way out is to raise the people.

How we see it: the risk in this zone is buying a platform first because a competitor announced one. Nothing changes until people can use it.

How we help: we start small and with people. The AI Readiness Assessment shows where to begin, and what not to buy yet. AI for Leaders gives the leadership team a position it can defend and the first three moves, and a Team AI Residency gets a first team using AI on their own work, with judgement (see Build Fluid Minds). And we help you write the one page of guardrails, approved tools and what data goes where, that lets people try things safely (see the governance dimension). Each of these is a complete piece of work on its own; none of them ties you to anything bigger.

#### 3.5 The Frustration Zone

The Frustration Zone is where an organisation stands when its people are ready to use AI but the organisation is not set up to support them. When people are ready but the organisation isn’t, AI adoption stalls.

What it looks like: AI capability exists, organisational support doesn’t. People know how to use AI, but without clear guidelines, approved tools, integrated workflows and clear goals, adoption remains informal and fragmented.

What it feels like: potential is visible, progress is stuck. People can see where AI could make a difference, but isolated experiments rarely scale into meaningful change. Individual capability has grown faster than organisational readiness.

What it costs you: innovation depends on individuals, not the organisation. Skilled people lose momentum and may leave. Shadow AI creates risks the organisation cannot adequately monitor. Improvements are inconsistent, because they depend on individual initiative rather than a repeatable, scalable approach.

In plain words: your people are ahead of your systems. More training does not help here. The way out is to fix the organisation.

How we see it: this is the situation that brings many organisations to the second and third moves. The skill exists; the rules, the workflows and the ownership stop it. Another training programme makes it worse, because it raises expectations the organisation still cannot meet.

How we help: we fix the organisation one process at a time. The Workflow Rebuild takes one everyday process, rebuilds it around your people and AI, names an owner and measures adoption from the first week (see Build Intelligent Organisations). The AI Design System sets the rules for how your AI looks and behaves, so every team can ship without a central team policing them (see Build Intelligent Organisations). On governance, we help you replace shadow AI with an on-ramp: approved tools with guardrails, and a clear path to say yes to a new use (see the governance dimension). And a Team AI Residency turns the individual skill your people already have into a team practice everyone can explain to a new joiner (see Build Fluid Minds).

#### 3.6 The Wasted Opportunity Zone

The Wasted Opportunity Zone is where an organisation stands when it has invested in AI tools, policies and strategy, but its people are not ready to use them. AI tools don’t create value until people use them.

What it looks like: the infrastructure is ready, the people aren’t. The organisation has invested in AI tools, policies and strategy, but employees lack the skills, confidence or motivation to use them effectively.

What it feels like: the tools are available, adoption is missing. Expensive tools sit idle as people struggle to use them or question their value. Technology has moved ahead, but the workforce has not been brought along.

What it costs you: you pay for AI capability that never translates into impact. Recurring tool costs deliver little return, while mandating usage or adding more tools fails to address the real barrier. Organisations see little improvement because the people are not equipped to change how they work.

In plain words: you have already paid for this and it is not being used. Another tool or a mandate from the top tends to make it worse. The way out is to raise the people.

How we see it: the platform is a sunk cost and the licences are a running one. The barrier is not the tool; it is skills, confidence and motivation. A mandate without reassurance creates uncertainty, not adoption (see the four stages of behaviour change).

How we help: we raise the people on the tools you already own. A Team AI Residency builds the capability to think, create and decide with AI inside a team, on your own work and your own platforms, and the Internal AI Academy spreads it past the first team (see Build Fluid Minds). We design the adoption itself, through the four stages of awareness, intention, action and repeat use (see the four stages of behaviour change), so the first attempt is easier than the old way and learning is rewarded. And we take a usage and outcome baseline on day one, so you can see the licences start to earn their cost (see the value realisation dimension).

#### 3.7 The Transformation Zone

The Transformation Zone is where an organisation stands when its people are ready and the organisation supports them. Readiness unlocks AI adoption. Alignment unlocks transformation.

What it looks like: people and systems are ready to turn AI into impact. People know when and how to use AI in their work. The organisation provides the tools, data, governance and workflows to support usage at scale.

What it feels like: from individual adoption to collective progress. People and systems evolve together. AI becomes embedded in how work gets done, decisions are made and teams collaborate. This is where adoption becomes transformation.

What it costs you: measurable value today, new possibilities for tomorrow. AI delivers lasting improvements in productivity, consistency and customer experience. Teams move beyond asking how to adopt AI to exploring what they can redesign, build and achieve with it.

In plain words: you have built it. The question now is what it is for. The way out is to build the new thing.

How we see it: readiness is a question about today. An organisation here has finished answering it, and the next question is what to build that was not possible before.

How we help: we build the new thing with you. Build Emergent Products designs and builds the product as one studio, with a working first version in two to four weeks and trust designed in from the first sketch. The AI Design System keeps many teams shipping coherent, trusted AI without central policing, and AI Disclosure and Oversight Design makes that AI explainable to a customer and an auditor (see Build Intelligent Organisations). The Internal AI Academy installs your own trainers, rhythm and governance, so the capability keeps growing without us (see Build Fluid Minds). And value realisation is how we agree we did: a measure on day one and the same measure at the end (see the eight dimensions of AI maturity).

Each zone has a way out, and the last one is building something new.

A founder skips the diagnosis and starts with the thing that pays for itself.

How we help, whichever zone you are in: we tell you which zone you are in and which dimension is holding the rest back, we say plainly what not to buy yet, and we make one move at a time. In the zones where an organisation is already spending, the obvious purchase (a licence, more training, another tool, a mandate) usually makes the problem worse, and saying so in the first meeting is the most useful thing we do.

#### 3.9 The eight dimensions of AI maturity

AI maturity isn’t one number. It’s eight dimensions that determine whether AI can deliver value. An organisation is never simply “doing AI” or “not doing AI”. It sits at different levels in different dimensions: it can be well ahead on data and nowhere on governance, and the weakest dimension holds back all the rest. We assess AI maturity across all eight to identify capability gaps, prioritise interventions, and translate AI investment into measurable business value.

The eight dimensions sit in three groups, which the framework calls indices. In one line: infrastructure to build on, activation to enable adoption, and value to prove it works.

| Group                 | The question it answers                 | Dimensions                               | What the group is                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
|-----------------------|-----------------------------------------|------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Infrastructure**    | Can the organisation support AI at all? | Data · Technology · Operations           | The foundations that make AI possible, reliable and scalable. Without them, everything else struggles: you can have a good strategy and skilled people, and nothing will scale if the data is out of reach or the work is not designed for AI. |
| **Activation**        | Can we put AI to work?                  | Strategy · Skills · Culture · Governance | The enablers: the organisational and human conditions that turn AI capability into everyday practice. This is where most organisations under-invest. They buy the technology and skip the conditions that decide whether it gets used.         |
| **Value realisation** | Is AI making a difference?              | Value realisation                        | The outcome measure, and a group of its own. Proof that AI is working: can you show measurable business impact, or are you only counting activity?                                                                                             |

The eight dimensions, their three groups and the questions in each one come from *The AI Maturity Benchmark* by Chris McKay and Jess Conser. The “what we do here” lines are ours.

##### Infrastructure group: data, technology and operations

The infrastructure group asks whether the organisation can support AI at all. Its three dimensions are the foundations: can AI reach your institutional knowledge, do you have the right systems and models for your needs, and is AI part of how work actually happens. Infrastructure is mostly an engineering job, and there are good people who do it. We work on it where it meets the work itself: operations is ours, technology is partly ours, and data is not ours.

##### Data (infrastructure dimension 1 of 8)

In one line: is your institutional data accessible to AI, securely and at scale?

What to look at: whether your data is stored in formats AI can use or scattered across teams, tools and inboxes; whether it is structured and organised so AI can understand it, including which document is the authoritative one when three disagree; and whether role-based access controls decide who, and which AI, may see what.

Where organisations stand: early, data is scattered across systems and not usable by AI. In the middle, some data is organised and connected, but access is inconsistent or risky. Mature, institutional data is structured, governed and AI-ready at scale. Most organisations are not short of data; they are drowning in it, unstructured and siloed. The journey is to centralise it, organise it, give AI governed access to it, and then treat it as the thing competitors cannot copy: everyone will have the same models, and only you have your context.

What we do here: data is not ours. When the main job is data (pipelines, platforms, cleaning it up), that is engineering work, and we say so.

##### Technology (infrastructure dimension 2 of 8)

In one line: do you have the infrastructure to deploy and scale AI across your organisation?

What to look at: whether you have access to appropriate AI models and applications for your different use cases; whether your AI tools work with your main applications rather than beside them; whether you have the infrastructure to manage AI agents centrally, including watching what they do; and whether you are locked into a single model or can swap in new ones as the market changes.

Where organisations stand: early, AI tools are fragmented and disconnected from core systems. In the middle, pilots are running, but they rely on brittle integrations and cannot scale across teams. Mature, the organisation has scalable infrastructure that supports AI agents, orchestration and interoperability. The authors’ advice: your job is not to stay on the frontier. If a model solves your problem well enough, use it; a multi-model approach is normal; and whether you build, buy or borrow depends on the problem you are solving, not the technology.

What we do here: technology is partly ours. We design how the AI behaves, what people see and how they stay in control; our engineering collaborator Trinos builds and integrates the platforms.

##### Operations (infrastructure dimension 3 of 8)

In one line: is AI embedded in how work actually gets done?

What to look at: whether AI is only a chatbot in a browser, or integrated into the tools you use and the flow of your work; whether you are automating existing tasks or rethinking whole workflows from an AI-native starting point; and whether you use AI to make decisions on real-time signals, not only on historical data.

Where organisations stand: early, AI is used manually and inconsistently, mostly as a personal productivity tool. In the middle, some tasks are automated, but most workflows have not changed. Mature, AI is embedded in operations with feedback loops and continuous improvement. The curve runs from AI assisting people, to automating tasks, to rethinking the process from first principles; bolting AI onto an unchanged process rarely works. Done well, it frees people’s capacity for judgement, critical thinking, creativity and empathy.

What we do here: operations is ours. It is the home of Build Intelligent Organisations: we take one everyday process and rebuild it with AI inside, with an owner, rules people follow and a number that moved.

##### Activation group: strategy, skills, culture and governance

The activation group asks whether you can put AI to work. Its four dimensions are the conditions that turn capability into everyday practice: is AI aligned to your business priorities, do people have the capability to use it, does your culture speed up or block adoption, and can you deploy it responsibly and at scale. Activation is a design and behaviour job, and almost nobody does it well. That is where we work. Strategy, skills and culture are ours outright; governance is partly ours.

##### Strategy (activation dimension 4 of 8)

In one line: how well is AI aligned to what actually drives your business forward?

What to look at: whether you can explain how AI connects to your business goals (growth, retention, efficiency, innovation); whether AI efforts are measured against real outcomes or only activity; and whether AI is a lever for your strategy or a standalone initiative.

Where organisations stand: early, AI work happens in silos with no clear connection to strategic priorities. In the middle, AI investments are directionally aligned to strategy but not yet measured. Mature, there is a clearly defined AI strategy that aligns to business goals and is communicated across the organisation. The authors’ rule: if AI is treated as the latest strategic objective, it is already going wrong. Business and talent goals come first, and AI strategy second. Mature leaders ask better questions: a chief financial officer asks what AI will do to the unit economics, not what the AI budget is.

What we do here: strategy is ours. The AI Readiness Assessment and the roadmap that follows it connect AI to the outcomes you already care about, and AI for Leaders in Build Fluid Minds builds that habit in the leadership team.

##### Skills (activation dimension 5 of 8)

In one line: do people have the capabilities they need to work with AI, and the confidence to use them?

What to look at: whether people are gaining real, hands-on experience with AI or only attending workshops; which capabilities matter more now that AI does parts of the work (judgement, adaptability, experimentation); and whether people are rewarded for learning and iterating, or only for getting things right the first time.

Where organisations stand: early, training is ad hoc or focused only on basic prompting. In the middle, some individuals are gaining experience, but capability-building is not systematic. Mature, the organisation is actively building the right mix of durable capabilities and technical confidence. The authors’ warning: organisations that train people on prompting are training for a moment that has already passed. The skills that work now are the human ones: judgement, critical thinking, decision-making and empathy, which build the confidence to know when to use the tools.

What we do here: skills are ours. This is Build Fluid Minds: AI for Leaders for the leadership team, the Team AI Residency for one team, the Internal AI Academy for the organisation.

##### Culture (activation dimension 6 of 8)

In one line: does your culture support experimentation, learning and change?

What to look at: whether it is safe to experiment, fail and share what you learned with AI; whether respected leaders model curiosity and adaptation, or control and perfectionism; and what behaviour is recognised: innovation and progress, or hours and output.

Where organisations stand: early, risk aversion is the norm and AI adoption feels unsafe. In the middle, signals are mixed: some teams embrace experimentation, others resist it. Mature, innovation and learning are visibly supported and celebrated. Culture starts at the top, with a message that says both things: nobody is being replaced, and everyone is expected to use it. It also runs through the respected veterans whose example others follow, and through what gets rewarded. Where hours are rewarded, people redo AI work by hand so it looks earned.

What we do here: culture is ours. The four stages of behaviour change are how we design for it, AI for Leaders is where we work on it with leaders, and the Team AI Residency is where it either takes hold in a team or does not (see Build Fluid Minds).

##### Governance (activation dimension 7 of 8)

In one line: can you deploy AI responsibly and at scale?

What to look at, in five parts: tool access (which AI tools people may use, on which accounts, and whether there is an approved list); data use (what data may be shared with AI tools, and who may access what); acceptable use (where AI is prohibited, and who approves a new use case, and how quickly); monitoring (whether AI use is tracked and reviewed, and who is responsible); and enforcement (what happens when rules are broken, who investigates, and whether consequences are documented and applied).

Where organisations stand: early, there are no clear policies and employees make their own decisions, so use is invisible and risky. In the middle, policies exist but are not well communicated or enforced. Mature, policies are clear and easy to find, use is monitored with clear oversight, and enforcement has assigned accountability. What good governance looks like, in the authors’ words: clarity (an employee can answer the five questions above in under thirty seconds), access (policies written in plain language and easy to find), updates (reviewed as AI capabilities change) and communication (people are told when tools or policies change).

Two things the authors stress. A policy document is not governance; governance is a living operating model with monitoring behind it, and it has to evolve as tools change their terms and models change their behaviour. And governance enforced too early, before people have the literacy to follow it, pushes AI use underground; the better path is a short set of guardrails and an on-ramp from the tools you do not want to the ones you do. One page that says what you can do, what you cannot, and who to ask works better than a hundred-page framework.

What we do here: governance is partly ours. We design the rules people follow and the screens that make them usable: AI Disclosure and Oversight Design and the oversight patterns in the AI Design System (see Build Intelligent Organisations), the governance rhythm installed by the Internal AI Academy (see Build Fluid Minds), and the governance work in Labs (see the governance work in Labs). Your legal, risk and IT teams own the policy itself and its enforcement.

##### Value realisation (dimension 8 of 8, a group of its own)

In one line: is AI making a difference, and can you prove it?

What to look at: whether you can demonstrate measurable business impact; whether you are capturing value or only activity; and whether you took a baseline before you started, so change can be shown.

Where organisations stand: early, the measures are activity (licences, log-ins, hours saved). In the middle, the measures are outputs: what the AI produced, such as a thousand product descriptions. Mature, the measures are outcomes tied to the business metrics you measured before AI, such as more conversions and fewer returns, against a baseline. The authors’ rule: the metrics that get an initiative funded are not the metrics that keep it funded. A year in, hours saved will not do; the business number has to move. Measure what you actually want, because people solve for whatever is measured. And the value of AI is wider than efficiency: capacity for more meaningful work, better collaboration, less burnout and better retention all count, if you choose to measure them.

What we do here: value realisation is how we agree we did. We take a measure on day one, before we change anything, and the same measure at the end; you see both. That is also why we price the outcome, not the hours.

##### Reading the eight together

The weakest dimension constrains everything else, so the first job is to find it. Three patterns come up often. Infrastructure solid but activation weak: you have capability but not the conditions to use it, which is the Wasted Opportunity Zone. Activation strong but infrastructure weak: people want to use AI but the foundation is not there, which is the Frustration Zone; here, more training will not help. Strong everywhere except value realisation: you are not measuring well, or your deployments are not aimed at the right outcomes. Different parts of one organisation also sit at different levels, so the plan is rarely one programme for everyone. The authors’ advice, and ours: do not try to fix everything at once. Pick the two or three dimensions where improvement will have the biggest impact, set specific targets, and measure again.

How we use it: the AI Readiness Assessment scores all eight dimensions, by team as well as for the organisation. We always report at least one dimension we do not work in, because a diagnosis that only recommends what we sell is not a diagnosis. Then the roadmap picks the two or three dimensions to move, and each one maps to a named piece of our work: skills and culture to Build Fluid Minds, operations and governance to Build Intelligent Organisations, value realisation to the baseline we take before anything changes.

What we learnt: AI investment creates potential. Maturity turns that potential into measurable value.

### 7. Our philosophy

#### 7.1 Why a benchmark is not enough

A benchmark can tell you where you are standing. It cannot tell you how anything moves. Organisations do not become capable because someone directs every movement. They become capable because the rules are shared.

The readiness zones (see the four readiness zones) are the diagnosis. Our philosophy is how change happens: one person, then a team, then the whole organisation. A client who only gets the diagnosis has a score and no idea what to do with it.

#### 7.2 Three ideas we build everything on

Intelligence is not only inside the individual. It emerges between individuals. One person learns to adapt. A team learns to think together. Then the whole organisation moves as one. That is the order we build in.

**Adaptive intelligence: one person, able to adapt.** One person’s ability to work through something new: learn it, question it, adapt, and create. It is the ability psychologists call fluid intelligence, which gives our company its name. For your people it means they know when to use AI, when not to, and whether its answer is good enough to act on. What it looks like: one person takes a real work problem and has something to show for it within a week. How we help: the Team AI Residency, where we build alongside one of your teams on their own work (see Build Fluid Minds), and, in a product, an interface that makes its user more capable rather than replacing their judgement (see Build Emergent Products).

**Collective intelligence: intelligence between people.** The hardest problems are too big for one mind. People, different disciplines and AI working together make an intelligence none of them has alone. For your people it means they share one way of working with AI that everyone uses and can explain to a new joiner. What it looks like: a team takes one real problem, works on it with AI, and writes down what worked so others can use it. How we help: the Team AI Residency, which turns one team’s individual skill into a shared practice (see Build Fluid Minds); one everyday process rebuilt around people and AI, with the handoffs between them designed (see Build Intelligent Organisations); and in a product, the trust layer between people and AI designed in from the start (see Build Emergent Products).

**Murmuration: what happens when adaptable people connect well.** No leader in charge. Each part responds to the parts around it, and the whole moves together, like a flock of birds. For your organisation it means agreeing a few simple rules for AI, so each team can act on its own and still move the same way, and people keep working the new way after we leave because everyone knows the rules. What it looks like: many teams using AI in the same trusted way, with no central team checking each one. How we help: we write the shared rules and hand them over. The Internal AI Academy installs your own trainers, rhythm and governance (see Build Fluid Minds); the AI Design System and AI Disclosure and Oversight Design are the rules many teams ship by (see Build Intelligent Organisations); and after a build, the design system and guidelines your team keeps building with (see Build Emergent Products). Leaving the capability behind is the last step of every engagement (see our method).

#### 7.3 How the zones and our philosophy fit together

The AI readiness zones and our three ideas are the same argument told twice, and putting them side by side is what makes both useful.

| Their term                  | Our term                    | Why they are the same thing                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
|-----------------------------|-----------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **AI literacy**             | **Adaptive intelligence**   | Literacy, at the size of one person. The literacy axis is not measuring how much somebody knows. It is measuring whether they can reason through something they have not met before.                                               |
| **AI maturity**             | **Collective intelligence** | What the maturity axis is trying to build. Data, operations, culture and rules are not ends in themselves. They are the conditions that let one person’s ability become something the whole organisation has.                      |
| **The Transformation Zone** | **Murmuration**             | People know how, when and which AI to use in the flow of their work, and it holds across many teams at once, without anyone directing every movement. That is coordination emerging from shared rules, which is what a flock does. |

The zones diagnose where an organisation is stuck. Our philosophy shows how intelligence can emerge.

#### 7.4 What a murmuration is

A murmuration happens when thousands of birds move together as one, with no leader directing them. Each bird responds to the birds around it, and from these simple interactions something emerges that is far more intelligent and beautiful than any single bird could create alone.

That made us wonder: what if human intelligence could work the same way? What happens when curious people, different perspectives, technology and AI begin to interact, not as separate minds but as a connected system? Perhaps intelligence is not only something we have. Perhaps intelligence is something we create together.

So: adaptive intelligence is individual adaptability. Collective intelligence is intelligence between individuals. Murmuration is what emerges when adaptable individuals connect well. Intelligence is not only what happens inside a mind. It is also what happens between minds.

We do not believe the future is about humans versus machines. We believe the future is about human intelligence amplified through intelligent systems. In time, the most powerful organisations will not be the ones with the most AI. They will be the ones that create the strongest relationship between people, AI, context and collective intelligence.

#### 7.5 Human-centred AI at three levels

AI affects more people than the one using it. So we design for three: the person using it, the people around them, and everyone it reaches. Design for the user. Then the community. Then society.

The three levels come from James Landay’s call for human-centred AI. We work the same way with you: your people first, then how your teams work, then the products you put into the world.

### 10. Fluid Intelligence Labs

#### 10.1 What Labs is

Fluid Intelligence Labs is the home for our own research, our experiments and the products we build for ourselves. We imagine a future where AI helps every person do work they are proud of. Labs is where we work towards it. We ask early questions, build small experiments, and share what we learn.

The idea behind Labs: as AI gets more powerful, the human side gets harder, not easier. Labs designs the human side of future AI: how people direct AI, work with it and keep a say.

Labs is not a service and nothing in it is for sale. It is where we try things before we offer them. When something built in Labs has been shipped for a client, it moves into our services. Designers, technologists and collaborators usually meet us here first: they come to see what we are building, then to work with us.

#### 10.2 How Labs works

Every Labs experiment follows four steps.

| Step                           | What it means                                                                                                |
|--------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Notice**                     | Something interesting is happening in how people and AI work together. We write down the question it raises. |
| **Build the smallest version** | The smallest thing that lets us see the question for real: a screen, a prototype, a short film or a demo.    |
| **Put it in front of people**  | Real people use it or watch it, and we learn what they understand, trust and do.                             |
| **Bring it back to the work**  | What we learn goes into how we design and build for clients, and into what we teach.                         |

We aim for each experiment to end in something you can see, not a paper.

#### 10.3 The four directions

Labs works on four things. The first two are things we are designing. The last two are questions.

|        | Direction                | The field                | In one line                                                                                        |
|--------|--------------------------|--------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **01** | The human in command     | AI agents                | A team of five people and fifty agents. What does its manager see? We are designing that view.     |

#### 10.4 The human in command (Labs direction 01, AI agents)

AI agents now act for you. Picture a team of five people and fifty agents. What does its manager see? That is the view we are designing in Labs: the control room where a person stays in charge. It shows what the agents are doing, what they may do on their own, when they hand over to a person, and how a person corrects or stops them.

This direction feeds our product work on Agent Systems, in Build Emergent Products: agents that do real work, with a person always in lead. The Labs work itself is research. It is not a service and you cannot buy it.

#### 10.5 Governance you can touch (Labs direction 02, every stage)

Most AI rules live in documents. We are designing screens for oversight, consent and audit: a screen to watch the AI, a screen to say yes or no, and a record to check. Rules people can use, at every stage of a product, rather than a policy nobody opens.

This direction feeds AI Disclosure and Oversight Design and the oversight patterns in our AI Design System, both in Build Intelligent Organisations. The Labs work itself is research. It is not a service and you cannot buy it.

#### 10.6 Work after AGI (Labs direction 03)

If AI can do most knowledge work, what is a job? What should a 14-year-old learn? This is a question, not an offering. We do not know the answer yet, and we do not sell one. KIWI, our free career-discovery app for Indian students in Classes 8 to 12, is a start: it helps a student choose a direction today, with honest information.

#### 10.7 Keeping a say (Labs direction 04, ASI)

If AI is smarter than us, how do people understand it, disagree with it and stop it? If AI does things we cannot check, how do people still have a say in what it does? This is a question, not an offering. It is the question behind everything else we do on trust: designing for the moment the AI is wrong, what the person sees, and how they can overrule it.

#### 10.8 Two terms, and the long view

AGI means AI that can do most knowledge work. ASI means AI that is better than people at almost everything. Neither has an agreed definition or date.

Our longer view runs in four steps: AI and generative AI, which is now; then agentic AI, where AI acts for you; then artificial general intelligence; then whatever comes after it. The last two are questions, not offerings. We are not selling readiness for something nobody can specify. We are saying which questions we intend to be good at.

This is why you will find that language in Labs and nowhere else. Our services are about what organisations can do with AI today.
